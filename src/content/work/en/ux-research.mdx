It's 11pm. A grad student is staring at a bug that won't die. The honest choice is three days of documentation, or one prompt into a chat window, and an answer in minutes.

<Figure src={img.uxChat} bare />

They pick the chat window. This time, it works.

Next time, the same **confidenttone** hands them a completely **wrong answer**, and they don't catch it until it's too late.

<Figure src={img.pic_ux_research_0} bare />

That gap — between **getting help** and **getting fooled** — is exactly where this research lives. Not whether grad students use AI. They all do. But **what's actually happening** in that invisible space in between.

<Section eyebrow="CONTEXT" heading="Why Grad Students, of All People?">

This started as a class project on how AI is reshaping different fields — each of us was assigned a segment to dig into. Mine was postgraduate computer science students: master's and PhD candidates studying abroad.

It turned out to be a good draw. This group is close to a best-case scenario for AI adoption — technically fluent, research-heavy workloads, constant daily exposure to AI for both academic and applied work. If anyone had a clear, lived sense of where AI genuinely helps and where it quietly fails them, it would be this group.

<Figure src={img.uxContext} bare />

</Section>

<Section eyebrow="METHOD" heading="Chasing Experience, Not Numbers">

What I needed wasn't a number. It was experience. Not just how often students use AI, but how it actually feels to use it: where it helps, where it quietly fails them, what they do when it lets them down. That kind of data doesn't live in a survey. It only comes out when someone talks, at length, in their own words. So the only real option was deep, one on one interviews.

It's worth mentioning the framework for choosing a research method that my UX Research instructor(Arghavan Saeedan) developed. In this approach, we started with the **research questions**, then identified the **type of data** that would best answer them, then chose the **research method**, and only after settling on these did we draft the actual user questions. Similar to the pattern below:

<Figure src={img.uxMethodEN} width={399} />

I started with a set of research questions I needed to answer, then worked backward from there: what data would actually answer them, what method would get me that data, and finally, what I could actually ask a person to get at their experience instead of a yes or no. Every question went through several rounds of review with my instructor before I asked it to a single student.

Once the questions held up, I ran the interviews with six students I already knew, which came with its own challenge: staying neutral with people you're close to takes more discipline, not less.

I went through the transcripts twice. Once with an Affinity Diagram, letting patterns surface from the raw data on their own. Once going back through, question by question, to make sure every original research question actually had an answer in what people told me. By the sixth interview, the patterns had stopped changing. That was the clearest sign it was time to stop and start synthesizing.

<Section eyebrow="FINDINGS" heading="Six Things I Didn't Expect to Hear So Clearly">

Six themes came out of the affinity mapping, clear enough that I didn't have to squint to see them. Here's what grad students actually told me, in their own words.

### 1. Specific Tasks, Not General Chat 🎯

Every student I talked to used AI constantly, but not as a general chatbot. Their use cases were specific: paraphrasing dense text, debugging code, summarizing academic papers with dedicated tools. They'd already outgrown the "ask it anything" mindset. What they wanted was speed and precision inside a workflow, not a chatbot to explore with.

> 🗯️❝ I often rely on AI to summarize articles or provide insights into complex topics, which saves me a lot of time.❞

### 2. The time saved is not small. It's dramatic 🪄

This wasn't a marginal efficiency gain. Students described AI turning multi day problems into a matter of hours.

> 🗯️❝ Instead of spending several days reading through documentation, I can resolve problems in just a few hours with AI.❞

This is the strongest, clearest value in the whole study, and also the highest bar anything else has to clear. Anything that adds friction is competing against days becoming hours.

### 3. Two very different walls: money and permission 🚧

The barriers weren't really technical. They were structural. Cost was one. Premium tools are expensive enough that students described pooling resources just to get access.

> 🗯️❝ It's quite expensive, but we're considering sharing an account to make it more manageable.❞

The other wall was institutional permission. Supervisors sometimes explicitly discouraged AI use, cutting students off from a tool they'd already come to depend on.

> 🗯️❝ My manager doesn't allow us to use AI tools for our projects, which makes it difficult for me to get help.❞

Together, these describe a group that wants in, but is being priced or policy blocked out.

### 4. Underneath the enthusiasm, a quiet worry about learning 🧠

Not everything was positive. A few students voiced something easy to miss if you only look at usage numbers: the worry that leaning on AI for answers might be quietly eroding their own understanding.

> 🗯️❝ I feel like I'm relying on AI for quick fixes rather than truly understanding the material.❞

The fastest answer isn't always the one people actually want, especially when learning is the point.

### Sounding Sure Isn't Being Right ⚠️

Students had learned, the hard way, that AI's tone has nothing to do with its accuracy.

> 🗯️❝ Sometimes, AI gives me confident answers that turn out to be completely wrong.❞

They've already internalized the need to double check everything. But right now, they're doing all of that verification work alone, with no help from the tool itself.

### 6. Worried about the job market, but not panicking 🤝

Given how central AI already is to their work, I expected more anxiety about job security. What I found instead was a kind of measured confidence.

> 🗯️❝ While I think the way we approach our work may change, I'm sure there will always be a place for humans in the workforce.❞

They expect their methods to shift. They don't expect to be replaced, and they still trust their own judgment more than the tool's.

</Section>

<Section eyebrow="THROUGH LINE" heading="What Do They Actually Want From AI?">

Put these six things together and one pattern sits underneath all of them. Students don't want AI to think for them. They want it to take the friction out of the parts of the work that were never really the point: formatting, searching, first drafts. And they want it to stay out of the way of the parts where the thinking itself is the value.

</Section>

<Section eyebrow="OPPORTUNITIES" heading="Where the Real Opportunity Is">

Take all six findings together, and the opportunity splits into three buckets: making access easier, making the tool a better partner in real work, and making sure it actually helps people learn instead of quietly replacing that effort.

**🧱 Making access easier**

- **Cheaper or tiered access.** Solves the cost barrier directly, instead of pushing students toward sharing accounts informally.

**🤝 Making the tool a better partner**

- **AI that adapts to a specific person's history and feedback,** instead of resetting every conversation, fixing a lot of the repeated friction students described.
- **Built in confidence and verification signals,** flagging where an answer is uncertain instead of stating everything in the same tone.

**✍🏻 Making it a partner in learning, not a shortcut around it**

- **Step by step problem solving,** especially for coding and research, pushing back against the exact pattern students worried about: getting the answer instead of understanding it.
- **An "explain it back" check.** Before moving to the next question, ask the student to summarize the answer in their own words, a quick way to catch whether they actually understood it or just copied it.
- **Progress tracking over time.** Show which concepts a student still leans on AI for, versus which ones they've genuinely picked up.

<Figure src={img.uxOpportunities} bare />

The through line stays the same here too. None of this is about AI doing more of the thinking. It's about AI getting out of the way faster, so the thinking stays where it belongs.

</Section>

<Section eyebrow="TAKE AWAY" heading="What This Project Taught Me">

**Staying neutral is a skill, not a default.** Interviewing people you already know takes more discipline than interviewing strangers, not less.

**A research question is only as good as your plan for answering it.** Once I knew I needed experience, not numbers, everything else about the method followed from that.

**Saturation is something you feel.** Watching the sixth interview reinforce the same six themes, not add new ones, was the clearest confirmation the sample size was right.

**The finding I trusted least turned out to be true for me too.** I did the theme extraction the traditional way: days of full time work, sorting quotes by hand. Later, for an unrelated task, I gave the same raw data to ChatGPT, just asking it to organize and clean it up. I never asked it to find themes. It grouped the data into almost the exact same six patterns anyway, in minutes.

</Section>

<Section eyebrow="AN UPDATE, MONTHS LATER" heading="The Worry That Got an Answer!">

A few months after this research wrapped, one worry actually got an answer. OpenAI released **Study Mode**, a version of ChatGPT that asks guiding questions instead of handing over the answer. Not long after, Google released **NotebookLM**, built around the same idea: understanding the material, not just getting through it faster.

Neither was built because of this study, but they're aimed at exactly what participants were worried about: that leaning on AI for quick answers was quietly costing them their own understanding.

The other findings haven't aged the same way. The cost barrier is only partly lower, cheaper plans exist now, but so does account sharing, still. And the confident, sometimes wrong answers participants described back then? Still confident. Still sometimes wrong.
