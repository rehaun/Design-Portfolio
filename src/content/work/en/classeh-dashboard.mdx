<Section eyebrow="CONTEXT" heading="The Same Screen, Three Different People">

A teacher opens Classeh to assign homework and check who's falling behind.  A student opens it to *do* that homework, and maybe sneak in a game.  A parent opens it just to know: is my kid okay?

Three completely different reasons to log in. One identical dashboard greeted all of them — a flat grid of look-alike tiles, nothing prioritized, nothing personal. Whatever you actually came for, you had to go hunting for it.

That grid is where this story starts.

<Section eyebrow="RESEARCH" heading="Starting Without a Map">

Here's the catch: I didn't have direct access to students, teachers, or parents. No interviews, no surveys, no seat next to them while they worked. So I started with the only honest thing I could: my own assumptions, based on months of living inside this product:

- **Students** come to *do* something, homework, tests, games.
- **Teachers** come to *assign and follow up*.
- **Parents** come to *stay informed*.

Assumptions aren't research, though. I needed something to check them against.

**Microsoft Clarity** gave me the first signal, the real ranking of most-visited pages: dashboard, then messages, then homework. That order later became the backbone of the navigation itself.

<Figure src={img.dashClarity} width={320} caption="Microsoft Clarity's most-visited pages — The most-visited pages I saw here later shaped which items got a more accessible spot in the navigation." />

Then I found a better proxy than any dataset: the **head of the support team**. She hears user frustration every single day, secondhand from the people I couldn't reach directly. I sat down with her, walked through my assumptions one by one, and let her tell me where I was right, and where I wasn't.

<Section eyebrow="WIREFRAME" heading="Sketching the Shape of Three Dashboards">

With 17+ existing modules and three very different sets of priorities, the question wasn't *what* to build, it was *how to group it* so each role saw their world, not everyone else's.

Working module by module with the support lead, I sorted tiles by what people were actually trying to accomplish, not by how the system happened to file them internally.

That gave me a rough map for three sketches, one per role, same skeleton, different priorities:

<Section eyebrow="RAPID PROTOTYPE" heading="The Idea That Got Killed in One Sentence">

My first real attempt at solving this was a **sidebar**, a rough version, shared early instead of perfected alone. The CTO shut it down instantly:

> "I know this sidebar model is outdated and won't work for us."

No explanation followed. And, in the moment, I didn't ask for one. But not everything in it was wrong. The shortcuts inside the sidebar were approved, so I kept them and just changed where they lived.

<Section eyebrow="TAKEAWAY" heading="What I'd Do Differently">

**A rejection without a reason leaves a question open, even when you agree with it.**

I wasn't unhappy with the outcome. I had my own doubts about the sidebar before the CTO said anything, and his rejection **matched what I already felt**. But to this day, I don't know if it was actually wrong, or just not **our taste**. He never said, and I never asked.

Since then, I've learned to **close that gap** before moving on, even when I already agree with the call. Asking *why* isn't about doubting the outcome, it's about not carrying an unresolved question into the next decision.

My **next step** in learning is figuring out how to get the answer to that question without creating tension or bad feelings. Hard human skills! :)

</Section>
